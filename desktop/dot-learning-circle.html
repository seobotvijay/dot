    NEP 2020 and Pre-Primary Schools: What It Actually Changes

    A plain-language guide to what India's National Education Policy (NEP) 2020 means for pre-primary schools, and what an NEP-aligned curriculum needs to include.

    NEP 2020 — India's National Education Policy, released in 2020 — is the first national policy to formally bring children aged 3 to 8 into the school system as a single "Foundational Stage." For pre-primary schools, that's not a minor footnote: it changes what a curriculum for 3–6 year-olds is expected to look like, and who owns responsibility for getting it right.

    This guide covers what actually changed, in plain language, without assuming you've read the full policy document.

    The 10+2 structure is gone

    For decades, Indian schooling was described as 10+2: ten years of school followed by two years of senior secondary. NEP 2020 replaces that with a 5+3+3+4 structure:

    • Foundational Stage (5 years): 3 years of pre-primary/preschool + Grades 1–2
    • Preparatory Stage (3 years): Grades 3–5
    • Middle Stage (3 years): Grades 6–8
    • Secondary Stage (4 years): Grades 9–12

    The key shift for pre-primary schools is the first bracket. Ages 3–6, which used to sit outside the formal school system in most policy conversations, are now explicitly Year 1–3 of a child's schooling — grouped together with Grades 1 and 2, not treated as a separate "before school" phase.

    What NEP 2020 expects from the Foundational Stage

    NEP 2020 and the related National Curriculum Framework for Foundational Stage (NCF) are specific about how these years should be taught, not just that they should exist. The broad expectations are:

    • Play-based and activity-based learning, not textbook-driven instruction
    • Multiple areas of development addressed together — language, cognitive, motor, social-emotional, and creative — rather than literacy and numeracy in isolation
    • Formal reading and writing introduced gradually, generally not before age 5–6
    • Trained educators for early years, recognizing that teaching 3–6 year-olds is a distinct skill from teaching older grades

    For a pre-primary school, this means a curriculum built around worksheets and rote memorization doesn't just feel outdated — it's misaligned with the stated national framework your school may eventually be evaluated against.

    What this means for your curriculum, in practice

    If you're evaluating (or building) a pre-primary curriculum with NEP 2020 in mind, three things matter most:

    1. Coverage across multiple developmental areas, not just early literacy and numeracy. A curriculum organized around a handful of narrow subjects won't reflect the whole-child approach NEP calls for.
    2. A play-based delivery model — daily activities, not worksheets, as the primary teaching method.
    3. Structured teacher training, since NEP's expectations only translate into classroom practice if educators are trained to deliver play-based learning, not simply handed a textbook.

    DOT's curriculum was built around these three points directly — it covers 8 areas of learning delivered through play-based, day-to-day classroom activities, mapped across four program levels from LKG through Std 2, with teacher certification built in rather than assumed.

    The bottom line

    NEP 2020 doesn't just add new terminology to early childhood education — it puts pre-primary schooling on the same policy footing as the rest of the school system, with specific expectations about how those years should be taught. Schools that treat pre-primary as a lighter, less structured stage are increasingly out of step with where the policy — and parent expectations — are heading.

    If you're reviewing your school's current pre-primary approach against NEP 2020, see how DOT's curriculum is structured or get in touch to talk it through.

    Does NEP 2020 apply to pre-primary schools?

    Yes. NEP 2020 is the first Indian education policy to formally bring ages 3–8 into the school system as the "Foundational Stage," covering 3 years of pre-primary (anganwadi/preschool) plus Grades 1–2.

    What is the 5+3+3+4 structure in NEP 2020?

    It replaces the older 10+2 structure. The first "5" is the Foundational Stage (3 years pre-primary + Grades 1–2), followed by 3 years Preparatory (Grades 3–5), 3 years Middle (Grades 6–8), and 4 years Secondary (Grades 9–12).

    Is a play-based curriculum required under NEP 2020?

    NEP 2020 and the NCF for Foundational Stage both call for play-based, activity-based learning rather than formal, textbook-driven instruction in the Foundational Stage, with formal literacy and numeracy introduced gradually from around Grade 1.

    8 Areas of Early Childhood Development Every Pre-Primary Program Should Cover

    A breakdown of the 8 areas of development a whole-child pre-primary curriculum should address — language, literacy, numeracy, social-emotional skills and more.

    Ask most parents what their child "learns" in preschool, and the answer is usually letters, numbers, and maybe some colors. That's not wrong — but it's a narrow slice of what a well-designed pre-primary curriculum should actually cover.

    Early childhood research is fairly consistent on this point: a 3–6 year-old's development isn't a set of separate subjects. Language, motor skills, emotional regulation, and cognitive growth develop together, and a curriculum that only targets early literacy and numeracy is leaving real developmental ground uncovered.

    Here are the 8 areas a genuinely whole-child pre-primary program should address.

    1. Language and Communication

    Vocabulary, listening skills, and the confidence to express a thought out loud. This is built less through direct instruction and more through conversation, storytelling, and dramatic play — a child narrating their own pretend-play scenario is doing real language development work.

    2. Literacy and Phonics

    Sound awareness, letter recognition, and the early building blocks of reading and writing. Critically, this should be sequenced after strong language foundations, not instead of them — phonics taught in isolation, without the vocabulary and listening skills to support it, tends not to stick.

    3. Numeracy and Maths

    Number sense, counting, pattern recognition and early problem-solving — introduced through hands-on, playful exploration (sorting objects, comparing sizes, simple games) rather than worksheets and rote counting.

    4. Me and My World

    Awareness of self, family, community and environment. This is where a child starts to make sense of who they are and how they relate to the people and world around them — often overlooked, but foundational to everything else on this list.

    5. Social and Emotional Learning

    Empathy, self-regulation, sharing, and friendship skills, usually built through circle time, guided group play, and adults modeling how to name and work through feelings. This area matters more than it's often given credit for: a child who can't yet self-regulate will struggle to focus on literacy or numeracy activities, regardless of how well those are taught.

    6. Art and Creative Expression

    Open-ended art, music, and imaginative play, where the process matters more than the output. This is one of the few areas where there's no "correct" answer — and that's the point.

    7. Movement and Coordination

    Fine motor skills (holding a crayon, using scissors) and gross motor skills (running, balancing, climbing), built through daily movement, games, and structured physical play. Fine motor development in particular is a direct prerequisite for handwriting later on.

    8. Health and Safety

    Everyday habits and awareness — hygiene, nutrition, and personal safety — built into classroom routines rather than taught as a one-off lesson.

    Why cover all 8 together, not separately

    The case for whole-child development isn't just philosophical. These areas reinforce each other in practice: a child with stronger social-emotional regulation engages better with literacy activities; a child with stronger fine motor skills has an easier time with early writing; a child with a richer vocabulary picks up phonics faster. Treating these as eight disconnected subjects misses how a 3–6 year-old actually develops.

    This is the structure DOT's curriculum is built around — all 8 areas of learning woven into a single play-based classroom experience, rather than delivered as separate subjects. See how it's sequenced across program levels, or talk to us about bringing it to your school.

    What are the main areas of early childhood development?

    A whole-child early years curriculum typically covers language and communication, literacy and phonics, numeracy and maths, awareness of self and community, social and emotional learning, art and creative expression, movement and coordination, and health and safety.

    Why shouldn't a preschool curriculum focus only on literacy and numeracy?

    Early childhood research consistently shows that language, motor, social-emotional and creative development are interdependent — a child's social-emotional regulation, for example, directly affects their ability to focus and learn early literacy and numeracy skills.

    How is social-emotional learning taught in pre-primary?

    Typically through structured routines like circle time, guided group play, and adult-modeled conflict resolution, rather than as a standalone lesson — it's built into how the classroom day runs, not a separate subject.

    LKG vs UKG: What Changes Developmentally Each Year

    What actually differs between LKG and UKG beyond the label — developmental expectations, curriculum focus, and what should carry over into Std 1.

    LKG and UKG are sometimes talked about as if they're the same year repeated twice — "preschool," broadly. Developmentally, that's a mistake. A 3-year-old and a 4-year-old are working with meaningfully different capabilities, and a curriculum that doesn't reflect that is either too advanced for LKG or not enough for UKG.

    Here's what should actually differ between the two years.

    LKG (Little Dot): first steps into structured learning

    LKG is typically a child's first sustained experience with a structured classroom — sitting in a group, following simple routines, and separating from a parent or caregiver for part of the day. At this stage (roughly ages 3–4), the priority isn't academic content. It's:

    • Building comfort with classroom routines and group settings
    • Early language development through conversation, songs, and stories
    • Fine and gross motor development through play
    • First exposure to numbers, letters, and shapes — through play, not instruction

    Pushing formal literacy or numeracy instruction too early in LKG tends to backfire. At this age, a child's attention span and fine motor control usually aren't ready for sustained writing practice, and forcing it can create early negative associations with "schoolwork" that are hard to undo.

    UKG (Big Dot): deeper exploration, still play-based

    By UKG (roughly ages 4–5), children have more classroom experience and stronger attention spans. This is where the curriculum should get more deliberate — not more formal, but more structured — across all areas of learning:

    • More structured phonics and pre-reading activities, building on LKG's language foundation
    • Number sense and early pattern recognition, moving beyond simple counting
    • Longer, more complex group activities and cooperative play
    • Growing independence in routines (managing their own materials, following multi-step instructions)

    The mistake schools make here often isn't adding more structure — it's swapping play-based delivery for worksheets in the name of "getting ready for Std 1." NEP 2020 and the NCF for Foundational Stage are explicit that UKG should still be play-based; what changes is the depth and complexity of what's being explored, not the format.

    What should carry into Std 1

    Under NEP 2020's 5+3+3+4 structure, Std 1 is still part of the same Foundational Stage as LKG and UKG — not a separate, more "serious" phase. The habits and skills built in UKG should carry forward directly:

    • Sound awareness and early reading skills built on in UKG should extend into formal literacy in Std 1
    • Number sense should extend into structured early maths
    • Fine motor control built through UKG activities should support the transition into regular handwriting
    • Classroom routines and independence should make the shift into Std 1 feel like a continuation, not a reset

    A curriculum that treats LKG, UKG and Std 1 as one continuous developmental arc — rather than three disconnected years — makes that transition far smoother for both children and teachers.

    This is exactly how DOT sequences its curriculum: Little Dot (LKG) and Big Dot (UKG) build directly into Tiny Dot (Std 1), with the same 8 areas of learning deepening year over year rather than resetting. See the full program level breakdown.

    What is the age difference between LKG and UKG?

    LKG (Lower Kindergarten) is typically for children aged 3–4, and UKG (Upper Kindergarten) for children aged 4–5, though exact cutoffs vary by school and region.

    Is UKG more academic than LKG?

    UKG should build on LKG with more structured exploration across all areas of learning and a stronger foundation for formal literacy and numeracy, but it should still be delivered through play-based activities, not worksheets or rote drilling, per NEP 2020 and NCF guidance.

    What should carry over from UKG into Std 1?

    The habits and foundational skills built in UKG — sound awareness, number sense, fine motor control, and classroom routines — should carry forward directly into Std 1, which under NEP 2020 is still part of the same Foundational Stage, not a separate academic phase.

    How Progress Tracking Prevents Learning Gaps in Early Years

    Why regular, structured progress tracking in pre-primary classrooms catches developmental gaps early — and what a good early years tracking system should actually measure.

    Most pre-primary schools assess progress at fixed points — a mid-term check-in, a term-end report. That cadence works reasonably well for catching gaps that are already obvious. It's much less effective at catching the ones that aren't yet.

    By the time a developmental gap is visible enough to show up in a term-end report, a child may have been quietly struggling with it for weeks or months — and because early years skills build on each other, an unaddressed gap in one area (fine motor control, social-emotional regulation, phonics awareness) tends to compound into others.

    What "progress tracking" should actually mean in pre-primary

    For younger children, this isn't the same as testing. Meaningful progress tracking in early years looks more like:

    • Regular observation, not periodic testing. A teacher logging what they see during normal classroom activities, on an ongoing basis, rather than administering a formal assessment every few months.
    • Coverage across all areas of development, not just early literacy and numeracy. A tracker that only measures reading and counting misses the social-emotional and motor gaps that often show up first.
    • Visible to more than just the teacher. Parents and school leadership should be able to see the same picture the teacher sees, not just a summary at term-end.

    Why this catches gaps earlier

    The core advantage of regular tracking over periodic assessment is simple: shorter feedback loops. If a teacher is logging observations regularly across all developmental areas, a gap becomes visible within days or weeks of it starting to emerge — not months later, once it's compounded into something bigger.

    This matters more in early years than in any later stage of school. A 4-year-old who's quietly behind on fine motor development, for example, isn't just behind on "art" — that gap will show up again in handwriting readiness for Std 1 if it isn't caught and supported early.

    What happens after a gap is identified

    Tracking on its own doesn't help unless it leads to action. A useful early years tracking system should make it easy for a teacher to:

    1. See exactly where a specific child is progressing well and where they're not, across all areas — not just an overall score
    2. Get that signal early enough to intervene with simple, targeted support (not a formal remediation program — usually just more focused attention in that specific area)
    3. Keep parents in the loop with the same information, so home and classroom support are aligned

    Why is early progress tracking important in preschool?

    Because early childhood development moves quickly and unevenly — a gap in one area (like fine motor skills or social-emotional regulation) can affect a child's progress in others, and the earlier it's identified, the easier it is to address with simple, targeted support.

    What should a preschool progress tracker actually measure?

    A good tracker should cover developmental skills and all areas of learning together — not just early literacy and numeracy — logged regularly by the teacher rather than assessed only at term-end.

    How often should a child's progress be tracked in pre-primary?

    Regularly — ideally as part of routine classroom observation rather than a periodic formal test, so gaps are visible as they emerge rather than discovered only at a term-end review.

    Frequently asked questions

    Everything school leaders usually ask before partnering with DOT.

    What is the DOT program?

    DOT is a play-based early years program that pre-primary schools adopt as their curriculum. It covers 8 areas of learning across four program levels — Little Dot, Big Dot, Tiny Dot and Mighty Dot — and pairs the curriculum with teacher training, a parent engagement program and a teacher & parent app.

    Is DOT aligned with NEP 2020 and NCF?

    Yes. The DOT curriculum is designed in line with India's National Education Policy (NEP) 2020 and the National Curriculum Framework for Foundational Stage (NCF), so schools get play-based, whole-child learning that also maps to national early-years guidelines.

    What grades and ages does the DOT program cover?

    DOT covers four program levels: Little Dot for LKG (ages 3–4), Big Dot for UKG (ages 4–5), Tiny Dot for Std 1 (ages 5–6), and Mighty Dot for Std 2 (ages 6–7).

    What are DOT's 8 Areas of Learning?

    The 8 areas are Language and Communication, Literacy and Phonics, Numeracy and Maths, Me and My World, Social and Emotional Learning, Art and Creative Expression, Movement and Coordination, and Health and Safety.

    What learning materials come with the DOT program?

    DOT includes a full set of hands-on learning materials: 30+ weekly plans for Little Dots (LKG) and 32+ for Big Dots (UKG), 2,500+ hands-on activities and 500+ worksheets every year, 100+ original story books, 300+ original rhymes, age-appropriate workbooks, 20+ decodable readers, theme and phonics books, and interactive teaching and learning materials — plus the teacher app and guide and regular parent workshops.

    How does DOT's progress tracking system work?

    The DOT app includes a built-in progress tracker that regularly records each child's learning and progress in developmental skills and across all 8 areas of learning. It's designed to surface gaps early, so teachers can step in with the right support before a child falls behind.

    What training do DOT teachers receive?

    Every DOT teacher completes a foundational certification workshop before rollout, then continues with termly refresher sessions and ongoing 1:1 classroom mentorship from early-years specialists.

    How are parents involved in the DOT program?

    Through DOT's Parent Engagement Program, parents get weekly take-home activities tied to classroom themes, termly orientation workshops, real-time updates through the DOT app, and community events with other DOT families.

    How can a school partner with DOT?

    Schools can fill out the partnership form on this site. A DOT partnership specialist will then walk through curriculum, teacher training and rollout, typically starting with a free classroom demo before the school commits.
